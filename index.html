Dronaqua is a fleet of small floating robots that detect PFOS in urban water bodies, adsorb it with biochar, and send a live contamination map back to shore. Built by four UWCSEA students, in collaboration with Singapore's National Water Agency.

The problem

PFAS don't break down. Our way of finding them hasn't changed in decades.

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), the "forever chemicals", enter urban waterways through consumer goods, industrial runoff and waste. Once there they persist, bioaccumulate, and are linked to cancer and hormone disruption.

Today, finding them means a person taking a bottle of water to a centralised lab for solid-phase extraction and direct-injection analysis. It's accurate, but it's slow, expensive, labour-intensive, and gives a single point reading hours or days later. Contamination hotspots go unmapped, and treatment at scale means high heat or strong chemicals that create their own byproducts.

We focus on PFOS, the most prevalent PFAS in urban water samples, and on closing the gap between detecting it and doing something about it, at the water's surface, in real time.

How it works

Detect, treat, map. One drone, one pass.

Each drone carries the full pipeline on board. A swarm of them covers a water body the way a single lab sample never could.

Detect

A PFOS-selective molecularly imprinted polymer sensor, mobilised on screen-printed electrodes and read by a mini-potentiostat, measures PFOS at environmentally relevant levels straight from the water. Sensor sourcing and customisation are being done with Metrohm.

Treat

An onboard biochar cartridge adsorbs PFOS from the sampled water. Biochar was chosen over high-heat or chemical remediation because it is cheap, effective and produces no harmful byproducts.

Map

Every reading is GPS-tagged and timestamped, then relayed drone-to-drone over an ESP32 mesh network to a Raspberry Pi control station, which renders a live concentration heatmap for operators.

CAD model of the drone. Solar panel, electronics bay, biochar module below the chassis, float ring for stability.

The drone

Small, modular, and built from parts you can order.

Designed in CAD and assembled by hand. Keeping each unit cheap is the point: a fleet of twenty costs less than one afternoon of conventional lab work at scale, and adding coverage means adding drones, not infrastructure.

Power
Solar panel with charge controller and voltage regulator; battery-backed for cloud cover
Sensing
MIP / screen-printed electrode, mini-potentiostat readout
Treatment
Replaceable biochar adsorption cartridge
Compute & comms
ESP32 for motor control and mesh radio; GPS module; Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W control station
Build cost
S$600–800 per unit at prototype volumes (solar ~S$80, motors ~S$100, electronics ~S$70, materials S$100–150, plus sensor)
Materials
Biodegradable float, propeller and frame where possible

Simulation & control station

From sensor readings to a map operators can act on.

Before the first drone hit the water, we built a simulation of swarm coordination, mesh communication and data return, and the control-station software that turns readings into a concentration map. The output below is simulated data for a 20 m × 20 m sampling area: the hotspots are where a crew would be sent first.

The same pipeline becomes DronaData: a growing, timestamped record of PFOS levels across catchments that doesn't exist anywhere today.

TreatmentModerate; high heat and strong chemicals remediate PFAS but produce other harmful byproductsHigh; carbon adsorption can bring PFAS in contaminated samples to under 1.5% of the original
CostHigh; fixed infrastructure and per-sample lab feesLow; S$600–800 per drone, solar-powered, no transport
Deployment speedSlow; fixed infrastructure, 4 h+ per resultNear-immediate; drones cover large areas quickly over the mesh

Where it's weaker: not every water body is accessible to a floating drone, and data collection stops in extreme weather. Labs remain the confirmation step for regulatory readings.

Our story

What four students did in a year.

  • The gap

    A monitoring problem nobody was funding

    While interning with PUB and in NUS water labs, Belle saw that urban PFAS sampling was periodic, slow and centralised. Jacob, after years of water and sustainability work at UWCSEA, wanted to build rather than campaign. Vikram, Jacob, Belle and Alex founded Dronaqua to close it.

  • Recognition

    Conrad Challenge Finalists

    Our PFAS detection and remediation concept was recognised for feasibility and impact, and the team was invited to the Innovation Summit in Houston as finalists. Exam clashes kept us home, but the invitation itself was a milestone.

  • Funding

    Youth innovation grants secured

    GoMakeADifference, the IB Global Youth Action Fund, the Awesome Foundation and others funded component purchases for R&D and prototyping. Most of the drone's parts have been bought.

  • Community

    United Water Conference 2025

    Co-hosted by Vikram, Jacob and Belle at UWCSEA, with sessions on pollutants, urban catchments and data-driven remediation, and industry professionals brought in through the alumni network. A platform for the next generation's water solutions, not just ours.

  • Partners

    PUB, NUS, NTU, A*STAR and Metrohm

    Method guidance from university researchers, instrumentation support from Metrohm, and interest from PUB's leadership in supporting patent applications and scaling nationally. The partnerships also open access to restricted catchment areas for data collection.

  • 12 May 2025

    Incorporated in Singapore

    Dronaqua is a registered Singapore business (UEN 53503874K), with a patent on the integrated detect-map-treat architecture in progress.
